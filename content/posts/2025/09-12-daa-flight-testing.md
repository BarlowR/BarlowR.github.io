I recently finished leading a large Detect And Avoid (DAA) campaign, spanning HITL & aircraft integration, HITL testing, aircraft testing, data analysis and report writing for the FAA. As a part of the campaign, I wrote a post for my company's website, which [can be found here](https://reliable.co/blog/flight-testing-for-acas-x-detect-and-avoid-daa-standards). 
As a part of writing the blog, I went through a long string of revisions with suggestions from our CEO, VP of Airspace Integration, Director of Communication, and Legislative Affairs Strategist. With each round of edits, the writing became less my own and more a product of the company. Part of what was stripped out was quite literally my own voice- we chose to change the writing from first person to an objective tone, shifting into a "expert voice" instead of conveying experience. I think the blog post as published achieves the goals I intended, conveying that we are building the right DAA system for integration into the National Airspace System, but there's a (vain) piece of me that laments the loss of my own voice in there. I think this website is a good place to hold on to the first person elements of the blog post that got cut; I've included an edited version below. 

<hr> 


Like many of us in the aerospace industry, I grew up obsessed with airplanes. One of my first memories of commercial flight was a visit to my grandparents in Florida. I spent the flight glued to the window, loving every minute of it.  When I arrived in Florida, my grandmother picked my parents and I up, and in response to my exuberance, she shared that she was afraid of flying. I couldn’t understand why: to be fearful meant to be concerned over safety, and my young self couldn’t fathom a reason for that concern. In the modern era I grew up in, commercial aviation has been defined by safety. For everyone on that plane that took us to Florida, safe operation was the baseline assumption. Safety is the baseline assumption for all passengers, whose numbers have grown to [1.1 billion in 2024](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/by_the_numbers/air-traffic-by-the-numbers-FY2024.pdf). By some accounts, commercial air travel is the safest form of transportation. But despite the incredible record, recent high profile accidents challenge this assumption, and show that the industry must continue to strive for improvement; tragedies are still possible.

Aviation has the potential to grow dramatically in the coming decades. New technologies have created opportunities to commercialize novel airborne systems, posing new stakeholders to enter operation into the national airspace. At Reliable Robotics, we represent one such stakeholder as we work towards an STC that will convert the Cessna 208B Caravan into an Uncrewed Aircraft System (UAS). To improve safety for all stakeholders and allow for safe interoperation between existing and new systems, new technologies can also provide improved solutions.

One of these new technologies is the [Airborne Collision Avoidance System X](https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/projects/airborne-collision-avoidance-system-x) (ACAS X). ACAS X is a family of algorithms developed by the FAA in partnership with MIT's Lincoln Laboratories. ACAS X versions include ACAS Xu for autonomous fixed-wing aircraft, ACAS Xr for rotorcraft, and ACAS Xa, a replacement for the existing Traffic Alert and Collision Avoidance System II (TCAS II) systems equipped in most transport-category aircraft. ACAS X improves upon TCAS II with improved alerting and a reduction in unnecessary alerts, especially near airports, and brings safe separation and collision avoidance functions to more aircraft operating in the NAS. 

At Reliable Robotics, our uncrewed system Detect and Avoid (DAA) solution will utilize ACAS Xu, the uncrewed fixed wing variant of ACAS X. We are currently working on a contract with the FAA to provide validation data for consensus standards for the use of ACAS X based DAA systems onboard uncrewed systems in the NAS. With an integrated ACAS X based DAA system, we are running extensive Hardware In The Loop (HITL) testing, and are performing a flight test campaign comprising 50+ encounters. The data from this testing will inform the development of DAA system consensus standards and the FAA's adoption and integration of ACAS X based systems. 

Earlier this month during the [NTSB hearings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_obibHEafLo) for the DC midair, the US Army identified ACAS Xr, the rotorcraft variant of ACAS X, as part of its solution to ensure that tragedy will never happen again. As we complete this contract for the FAA and continue our work with industry and academic partners to further the integration of ACAS X into the national airspace, I am proud to be contributing to technology that I believe will enhance safety for all users of the NAS.
